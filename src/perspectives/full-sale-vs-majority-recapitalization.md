<h4><strong>Understanding the Two Main Types of SaaS M&amp;A</strong></h4><p>When a SaaS founder decides to pursue an exit or liquidity event, the two most common paths are:</p><p><strong>1. Full Sale to a Strategic Acquirer</strong></p><p>A full acquisition occurs when a larger company, typically a strategic acquirer, purchases 100% of the SaaS business. This buyer could be a competitor, a company looking to expand into a new market, or a business seeking to enhance its product offerings with your technology.</p><p>There is some nuance here, as many strategic acquirers are owned by private equity (PE) funds. In these cases, the PE firm often funds or “sponsors” the transaction as a “bolt-on” acquisition to its portfolio company.</p><p><strong>2. Majority Recapitalization by a Private Equity Firm</strong></p><p>A majority recapitalization (recap) involves selling a controlling stake (typically 51-80%) of the company to a private equity firm while the founder and management team retain an ownership stake. The PE firm provides capital for growth, professionalizes operations, and eventually seeks another exit, typically within 3-7 years.</p>

  

  

  <hr />
  
    

    

  

  
  <h4><strong>Key Considerations Before Choosing a Path</strong></h4><p>Both M&amp;A options offer unique advantages and trade-offs. Founders should assess several factors before making a decision:</p><p><strong>1. Liquidity Needs</strong></p><ul><li><p><strong>Full Sale:</strong> Maximizes immediate liquidity. Founders receive cash upfront but forfeit future upside.</p></li><li><p><strong>Majority Recap:</strong> Provides a partial cash-out while maintaining equity for a future exit. PEAK recently wrote an article about <a href="/perspectives/rollover-equity">rollovers</a>. </p></li></ul><p><strong>2. Risk Tolerance</strong></p><ul><li><p><strong>Full Sale:</strong> Eliminates financial risk but may result in loss of control and integration challenges.</p></li><li><p><strong>Majority Recap:</strong> Retains ownership risk but allows continued participation in the company’s future growth.</p></li></ul><p><strong>3. Cultural Fit</strong></p><ul><li><p><strong>Full Sale:</strong> The acquirer often imposes its own culture, processes, and leadership structure.</p></li><li><p><strong>Majority Recap:</strong> The founder generally retains influence, and PE firms typically support the existing culture while driving operational efficiencies.</p></li></ul><p><strong>4. Tax Implications</strong></p><ul><li><p><strong>Full Sale:</strong> Typically results in a single taxable event, usually at long-term capital gains rates.</p></li><li><p><strong>Majority Recap:</strong> Often structured to defer some tax liability, as founders roll over equity into the new entity.</p></li></ul><p><strong>5. Rollover Equity (“Second Bite of the Apple”)</strong></p><ul><li><p><strong>Full Sale:</strong> No future upside—founders receive full proceeds and exit.</p></li><li><p><strong>Majority Recap:</strong> Founders roll over 10-40% equity, often leading to a larger second payout when the PE firm exits.</p></li></ul><p><strong>6. Desire to Continue Working &amp; Future Role</strong></p><ul><li><p><strong>Full Sale:</strong> Typically requires a transition period (6-24 months) before the founder fully exits.</p></li><li><p><strong>Majority Recap:</strong> Founders often stay involved, leading growth with PE backing.</p></li></ul><p><strong>7. Employee Retention</strong></p><ul><li><p><strong>Full Sale:</strong> Acquirers may consolidate teams or restructure operations.</p></li><li><p><strong>Majority Recap:</strong> Employee equity incentives can be restructured, often improving retention.</p></li></ul><p><strong>8. Customer Retention</strong></p><ul><li><p><strong>Full Sale:</strong> Customers may experience service changes post-acquisition.</p></li><li><p><strong>Majority Recap:</strong> Business continuity is preserved, reducing customer churn risk.</p></li></ul>

  

  

  <hr />
  
    

    

  

  
  <h4><strong>Valuation Considerations</strong></h4><p>M&amp;A valuations for SaaS companies are highly subjective and vary based on growth, revenue retention, market conditions, and other factors.</p><p><strong>Full Sale Valuation</strong></p><ul><li><p>Typically ranges from <strong>6x-15x ARR</strong>, depending on strategic fit, synergies, and financials.</p></li><li><p>Acquirers may justify premium valuations if they can cross-sell or integrate products seamlessly.</p></li></ul><p><strong>Majority Recap Valuation</strong></p><ul><li><p>Private equity firms focus on revenue growth, scalability, and customer retention, with valuations usually in the <strong>5x-12x ARR</strong> range for high-quality companies.</p></li><li><p>Typically lower than strategic acquisitions, but the potential for a second exit ("second bite of the apple") can lead to a greater overall return for founders.</p></li></ul><h4><strong>Conclusion: Which Path is Right for You?</strong></h4><ul><li><p><strong>Choose a full sale:</strong> if you prioritize maximum liquidity, risk elimination, and a clean break.</p></li><li><p><strong>Choose a majority recap:</strong> if you want partial liquidity, continued leadership, and the potential for a second payout.</p></li></ul><p>Each founder’s goals are different, and the right M&amp;A strategy depends on personal and business objectives. Evaluating these factors with financial advisors and M&amp;A experts will help ensure the best possible outcome.</p>
