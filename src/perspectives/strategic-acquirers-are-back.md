<div class="row"><div class="col" style="--span: 12; --of: 12"><div class="b b--html"><p>Strategic corporate M&amp;A in enterprise SaaS surged at the end of 2025 (up 168.5% in Q4 2025 alone). PE sponsors were active. Strategics were dominant. Most founders building their exit thesis around financial sponsor math alone just got handed a reason to reconsider. </p><p>Strategic acquirers don’t buy your business in isolation. They buy what your business does inside theirs: revenue acceleration, capability gaps, data infrastructure, customer access. That logic doesn’t appear in a DCF, but it absolutely appears in the offer. PE sponsors run LBO math: they model debt service, entry multiples, and exit targets, and they back into a price. That math is disciplined, but it has a ceiling. A strategic with a genuine capability gap in your category can rationally clear that ceiling. The synergy premium can take your valuation somewhere the buyout math simply can’t follow. </p><h4><strong>IBM and Confluent: The Logic Made Concrete </strong></h4><p>IBM acquired Confluent for $11B at a 34% premium, all-cash, completed in March 2026. The deal had nothing to do with leveraged buyout math. IBM was buying AI data infrastructure (the real-time data plumbing that enterprise AI systems depend on). Confluent’s product is embedded in the workflows IBM needs to build the AI stack its largest customers are demanding. IBM wasn’t paying for Confluent’s standalone cash flows. It was paying for what Confluent unlocks inside IBM’s ecosystem. </p><p>That’s synergy value. And IBM wasn’t alone. Technology deal value in February 2026 surged 540% year-over-year, per EY M&amp;A Activity Insights. PwC’s analysis of the 100 largest corporate M&amp;A deals in 2025 found that within technology, nearly all cited AI as part of the strategic rationale. Strategics aren’t browsing. They’re hunting for specific capabilities. The hunting has intensified. </p><h4><strong>The Question Most Founders Aren’t Asking </strong></h4><p>Most exit preparation focuses on PE-readable metrics: Rule of 40, gross dollar retention, NRR, LTV/CAC. Those matter. No serious process ignores them. But optimizing exclusively for financial sponsor logic means entering a process with a partial buyer universe and a ceiling on the valuation conversation. </p><p>The right question isn’t just “what would a PE firm pay for this business?” It’s also: “Who in the strategic landscape has a capability gap my product fills, and what is my product worth inside their ecosystem?” Those two questions produce different answers. For well-positioned companies, the strategic answer is often meaningfully higher. </p><p>Understanding where your product fits in a strategic buyer’s build-vs-buy calculus is not a nice-to-have. It is exit preparation. The founders who identify motivated strategic buyers early (before a process launches) and show up to the table with both more leverage and more options. </p><h4><strong>Bottom Line </strong></h4><p>Strategics returned at scale in Q4 2025 and brought a different kind of checkbook. They pay for synergies, not just standalone cash flows, which means they can outbid PE when the right asset is in front of them. Founders who understand what their business is worth inside a strategic’s ecosystem (not just on a sponsor’s model) are the ones who walk away with the better number. </p><h4><strong>Sources</strong></h4><p class="sqsrte-small"><strong>Fortune/PitchBook, “The Death of SaaS Could Be the Best Thing for SaaS M&amp;A,” March 31, 2026</strong></p><p class="sqsrte-small"><strong>CNBC, IBM/Confluent acquisition coverage, December 2025</strong></p><p class="sqsrte-small"><strong>EY M&amp;A Activity Insights, February 2026</strong></p><p class="sqsrte-small"><strong>PwC Global M&amp;A Industry Trends 2026 Outlook, January 2026.</strong></p></div></div></div>
